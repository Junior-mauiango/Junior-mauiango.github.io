Research overview

As electric vehicle adoption grows, distribution systems have to support new patterns of electricity demand and, in some cases, bidirectional power flow between vehicles and the grid. My research looks at how these changes affect the operation of a distribution network.

I use MATLAB and MATPOWER to build time-series power-flow simulations and study how EV activity affects bus voltage, power losses, branch loading, and other grid conditions throughout the day.

My work has included two related areas: Vehicle-to-Grid operation, where EVs can return stored energy to the electrical system, and dynamic wireless charging, where EV charging demand has to be coordinated with the condition of the distribution network.

The distribution-grid model

The research uses the IEEE 33-bus radial distribution system as a test network. Using a standard distribution model gives me a consistent system for comparing different EV-grid scenarios and studying how changes at different parts of the feeder affect overall operation.

I developed MATLAB/MATPOWER simulations that evaluate the network across a full day rather than looking at only one operating point. The simulations run at five-minute intervals so changes in demand, EV activity, and network conditions can be observed over time.

At each point in the simulation, MATPOWER is used to solve the power flow. I then analyze results such as bus-voltage profiles, power losses, branch loading, and how those quantities change as EV activity changes.

Vehicle-to-Grid research

Vehicle-to-Grid, or V2G, allows electric vehicles to send stored battery energy back into the electrical grid when they are connected and available. This means EVs can act as distributed energy resources instead of only behaving as electrical loads.

One part of my research studies how V2G affects a distribution system during periods of higher electricity demand. I compare normal grid operation with scenarios where EVs are able to provide power back to the network.

The goal is not simply to determine whether V2G can provide power. The more important question is how that power changes conditions throughout the feeder and whether those changes create benefits or new operating problems.

Time-series EV modeling

EV behavior changes throughout the day, so the simulation does not treat every vehicle as being available at the same time. Synthetic EV activity is used to create changing conditions across the network during the simulation.

This makes it possible to study V2G as a time-dependent grid problem. The amount and location of EV power interacting with the feeder can change while the underlying electricity demand is also changing.

I use these simulations to compare how the same distribution system behaves under different EV-grid operating conditions without exposing the detailed research methods or unpublished model parameters.

What I analyze

The simulations produce several electrical measurements that help me understand how EV integration affects the distribution network.

  • Bus voltage: I analyze voltage profiles across the feeder to see how EV activity changes voltage conditions at different locations.
  • Power losses: I evaluate how different EV-grid scenarios affect real-power losses throughout the distribution network.
  • Branch power flow: I study how EV charging and discharging change the amount and direction of power moving through different parts of the feeder.
  • Network loading: I monitor how EV activity changes loading across the distribution system and where additional grid stress may appear.
  • Time-dependent behavior: I compare these measurements throughout the day instead of relying on a single power-flow snapshot.

What the V2G simulations showed

The simulations showed that V2G can help improve low-voltage conditions on a distribution feeder during periods of higher electricity demand.

However, the results also showed that simply increasing the amount of power supplied by EVs does not automatically improve overall grid performance. Too much uncoordinated power injection can create new problems, including overvoltage and increased loading in parts of the network.

This showed me that V2G needs to be coordinated with the condition of the grid rather than simply allowing every available vehicle to discharge whenever power is needed.

Key Finding

V2G can support the grid — but it has to be coordinated.

The simulations showed that EV discharging can improve voltage conditions during periods of higher demand. At the same time, uncoordinated power injection can create overvoltage and increase network loading. The results showed why V2G control has to consider the condition of the distribution system.

Looking beyond a single grid metric

One of the important things I found through this work is that improving one part of grid performance does not necessarily improve everything else.

For example, EV power injection can help support voltage in one part of the network while changing power flow and loading somewhere else. That means V2G has to be evaluated as a system-level problem rather than focusing on only one measurement.

This became one of the main ideas behind my research: EVs can provide useful flexibility to the electrical grid, but the way they are coordinated matters just as much as the amount of power they can provide.

Dynamic wireless EV charging

Another part of my EV-grid research looks at dynamic wireless charging, where electric vehicles receive energy while traveling through an equipped charging region.

My work focuses on the distribution-grid side of the problem rather than the physical design of wireless power-transfer hardware. I use MATLAB and MATPOWER to study how additional EV charging demand affects the feeder and how charging can be coordinated with grid conditions.

Because several vehicles may need energy while the distribution system is already supporting other loads, the grid cannot always treat every charging request independently. The charging strategy therefore has to consider both EV demand and the electrical condition of the network.

Grid-aware charging control

I worked on a grid-aware charging approach that manages EV charging demand based on the condition of the distribution feeder.

The goal is to allow EVs to receive energy while keeping the network within acceptable operating conditions. When additional charging would place too much stress on the feeder, some charging demand can be delayed rather than adding all of the requested load at once.

This allowed me to study how EV charging decisions and power-system conditions can be connected through a control strategy without treating the transportation load separately from the electrical grid.

Connecting both research areas

V2G and dynamic wireless charging move energy in different directions, but both are connected by the same engineering problem: how can a distribution system support a growing number of electric vehicles without creating new grid problems?

The V2G work looks at EVs as possible grid resources that can return energy to the electrical system. The wireless-charging work looks at EVs as changing electrical loads that need energy from the grid.

Working on both sides of the problem has helped me understand EV integration as more than just adding vehicle load to a power system. It is also a control and coordination problem involving transportation demand, grid conditions, and power-system operation.

My work

I developed the MATLAB/MATPOWER simulation framework, implemented the time-series power-flow analysis and EV-grid models, generated and analyzed simulation results, and evaluated how different EV scenarios affected bus voltage, power losses, and network loading.

The research has given me hands-on experience building power-system simulation tools, working with distribution test systems, processing time-series results, and turning simulation data into engineering conclusions.

Tools & technical skills

The research has involved MATLAB programming, MATPOWER power-flow simulation, IEEE distribution test systems, time-series modeling, synthetic EV scenarios, load-profile modeling, voltage analysis, power-loss calculations, network-loading analysis, data visualization, and interpretation of distribution-system behavior.

What I learned

One of the biggest things I learned from this research is that you cannot judge an EV-grid strategy using only one measurement. A change that helps voltage may affect losses, power flow, or network loading somewhere else.

The project also showed me why time-series simulation is important. Electricity demand, EV activity, and grid conditions change throughout the day, so looking at only one power-flow condition can miss problems that appear at other times.

Most importantly, this work has helped me connect programming and simulation with the power-system concepts I have learned in electrical engineering. It has also given me experience working on a research problem where the answer is not known before the simulation is built and analyzed.
