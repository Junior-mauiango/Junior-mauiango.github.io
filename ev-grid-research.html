Research overview

As electric vehicle adoption increases, distribution networks must support a growing amount of power being transferred between vehicles and the grid. My research focuses on understanding how these new loads and distributed energy resources affect voltage, power flow, system losses, and network loading.

I use MATLAB and MATPOWER to build time-series simulations of distribution systems under different EV integration strategies. Rather than evaluating the grid at only one operating point, the simulations model changing conditions throughout a full 24-hour day.

My work has included two related areas of EV-grid integration: Vehicle-to-Grid operation, where parked EVs can discharge stored energy back into the distribution system, and coordinated dynamic wireless charging, where EV charging requests must be managed while vehicles receive energy on the move.

The distribution-grid model

The research uses the IEEE 33-bus radial distribution network as the main test system. The network provides a controlled environment for studying how changing loads and EV power injections affect different locations across a distribution feeder.

For the V2G study, a daily load profile was applied to the network to represent changing electricity demand. The simulation then progressed through 288 five-minute intervals covering an entire 24-hour period. At every interval, the bus loads were updated and a MATPOWER power flow was executed.

From each power-flow solution, I recorded quantities such as bus voltage magnitude, branch power flow, real-power losses, and network loading. This allowed changes to be studied across both location and time rather than looking only at total system demand.

Vehicle-to-Grid research

Vehicle-to-Grid technology allows an electric vehicle to operate as more than a conventional electrical load. With bidirectional power flow, energy stored in the EV battery can be returned to the grid when the vehicle is connected and available.

I investigated how this behavior could affect a distribution network during periods of high electricity demand. The V2G study compared a baseline IEEE 33-bus system against a scenario where a portion of a synthetic EV fleet injected power back into the network during the evening peak period.

01

Generate the EV fleet

A synthetic fleet of 700 EVs was distributed across the network with randomized availability characteristics to represent varying vehicle behavior.

02

Enable V2G participation

Thirty percent of the fleet was designated as V2G-capable. Available vehicles injected power into their connected buses during the evening peak period.

03

Run power flow

At every five-minute interval, MATPOWER solved the updated network and the resulting voltages, branch flows, losses, and overload conditions were recorded.

Modeling realistic EV behavior

The V2G simulation used a synthetic fleet rather than assuming that every vehicle behaved identically. Vehicles were assigned different arrival times, dwell durations, locations, and discharge capabilities. This created changing V2G availability throughout the simulation.

For this specific study, charging was intentionally excluded from the V2G scenario. The purpose was to isolate the effect of EV power injection and directly compare distribution-system behavior with and without V2G discharging.

During the defined evening peak period, V2G-capable vehicles that were available at their assigned buses reduced the local net demand by injecting power into the grid. Once the V2G period ended, the system returned to its normal baseline behavior.

What I measured

The simulation tracked several electrical quantities to evaluate both the potential benefits and the unintended consequences of V2G.

  • Bus voltage: voltage magnitude was tracked across the distribution network to identify undervoltage and overvoltage conditions.
  • Real-power losses: branch losses were calculated at each simulation interval.
  • Branch power flow: power flowing through individual sections of the feeder was monitored as V2G changed the direction and magnitude of network power flow.
  • Thermal loading: branch flows were compared against the assumed network rating to identify overload conditions.
  • Time-dependent behavior: all measurements were stored across the 24-hour simulation so that peak-demand behavior could be compared against off-peak conditions.

What the V2G simulation showed

The baseline simulation showed the greatest voltage stress during the evening demand peak. Without V2G, the lowest observed bus voltage reached approximately 0.92 per unit.

Introducing V2G changed that behavior significantly. During the discharge period, local EV power injection reduced the voltage drop experienced at many buses. The minimum voltage during peak operation increased, showing that distributed EV energy storage can provide voltage support when the network is heavily loaded.

The results also exposed an important limitation: more V2G power was not automatically better. In parts of the network, the additional injection pushed voltage well above its normal operating range, with the simulation reaching approximately 1.35 per unit in the most extreme case.

Key Finding

V2G can support the grid — but it must be coordinated.

EV discharging improved low-voltage conditions during peak demand, but excessive distributed injection also created overvoltage, increased losses on some branches, and produced network overloads. The results reinforced the importance of controlling when, where, and how much power EVs return to the grid.

Power-loss and network-loading tradeoffs

V2G also changed the distribution of real-power losses throughout the network. Under baseline operation, power primarily flowed from the substation toward downstream loads. When EVs began injecting power at different buses, the magnitude and direction of those flows changed.

In the simulated V2G case, some branches experienced substantially higher losses during the discharge period. Several branches also exceeded the assumed thermal limit, while the baseline simulation did not produce sustained overloads.

This was an important result because it showed that improving one grid metric does not guarantee improvement everywhere else. V2G improved the low-voltage problem, but uncontrolled or excessive discharge could create a different set of operating problems.

Dynamic wireless EV charging

Another part of my EV-grid research examines dynamic wireless charging, where electric vehicles receive energy while traveling through an equipped charging region. From the distribution-grid perspective, this creates a different problem from conventional charging because multiple vehicles may request power while the feeder is already supporting other loads.

My work focuses on the grid-control side of the problem rather than the physical design of wireless power-transfer coils. I model the charging demand in MATLAB/MATPOWER and investigate how charging requests can be coordinated to prevent unacceptable distribution voltage conditions.

Priority-based charging control

Instead of allowing every requesting EV to charge simultaneously, the control strategy evaluates which vehicles should be admitted based on charging priority and the electrical condition of the feeder.

Vehicles entering the charging region generate charging requests. Those requests are ranked according to their charging need. The highest-priority vehicles are considered first, but charging is only accepted while the resulting feeder voltage remains within the defined operating constraint.

As the system approaches its voltage limit, lower-priority requests are delayed rather than adding additional load to the feeder. This creates a direct connection between individual EV charging decisions and distribution-system operating conditions.

1

EV enters charging region

The vehicle requests power from the dynamic wireless charging system.

2

Charging requests are prioritized

Vehicles are ranked so that EVs with greater charging need can be considered before lower-priority requests.

3

Grid condition is evaluated

The charging load is evaluated against the distribution network to determine whether another vehicle can be admitted without violating the feeder voltage constraint.

4

Accept or delay the request

Charging continues to be allocated while the grid remains within its operating constraint. Lower-priority requests are delayed as the network approaches the voltage threshold.

Connecting both research areas

Although V2G and dynamic wireless charging move energy in opposite directions, both projects address the same broader engineering question: how can a distribution grid support a large population of electric vehicles without sacrificing reliable operation?

The V2G work demonstrates that EV batteries can become distributed grid resources, but also shows why their power injection must be coordinated. The wireless-charging work approaches the problem from the load side by controlling which charging requests the network can support.

Together, these studies have allowed me to explore EV integration as a power-system control problem rather than simply treating electric vehicles as fixed electrical loads.

Tools & technical skills

The research has involved MATLAB programming, MATPOWER power-flow simulation, IEEE distribution test systems, time-series modeling, stochastic EV behavior, load-profile modeling, voltage analysis, branch-loss calculations, data visualization, and interpretation of distribution-system operating constraints.

What I learned

One of the biggest lessons from this research is that grid integration requires looking at the entire system rather than optimizing a single metric. A strategy that improves voltage can simultaneously increase losses or overload another section of the feeder.

It also showed me the importance of time-series simulation. EV availability, electricity demand, and grid conditions are constantly changing, so a single power-flow snapshot cannot capture many of the problems that appear during a full day of operation.

Most importantly, the work strengthened my understanding of how software, control strategies, and power-system analysis can be used together to study emerging challenges created by transportation electrification.

This research is conducted through the University of Oklahoma Power Quality & Resilience Lab under the direction of Dr. Paul Moses.
