Analog Fire Detection Circuit

Project overview

This project focused on building a basic fire-detection prototype using analog electronics rather than programmable control.

The circuit used two separate environmental inputs. A thermistor responded to changes in temperature, while a light-dependent resistor provided an optical input that could be changed by reducing the amount of light reaching the sensor.

During testing, covering the photoresistor by hand was used as a simple way to reproduce a darker optical condition and observe how the sensor stage responded. This provided a practical way to test the optical portion of the circuit without using actual smoke.

The sensor signals were processed through analog circuitry and compared against selected thresholds. When the input conditions crossed the desired trigger level, the warning output activated.

Temperature sensing

The temperature-detection stage used a thermistor whose resistance changed as its temperature changed.

By incorporating the thermistor into a resistive sensing network, its change in resistance produced a corresponding change in voltage. This voltage could then be evaluated by the following analog stages.

This provided a simple method of converting a physical temperature change into an electrical signal that the rest of the circuit could process.

Optical sensing

The second sensing stage used a light-dependent resistor, or photoresistor.

The resistance of the photoresistor changes according to the amount of light reaching its surface. This allowed the circuit to respond to changes in illumination.

For testing, the sensor could be covered by hand to create a darker condition. The goal was not to claim direct smoke measurement, but to demonstrate how an optical sensor could provide a second independent input to an analog detection system.

Analog threshold detection

Because the project did not use software, the decision to activate an alarm had to be made entirely with analog circuitry.

The sensor voltages were processed through analog stages that compared their values against selected reference levels. Once the sensor signal crossed the desired threshold, the circuit changed state and activated the output.

This made the project a useful exercise in understanding how an analog system can implement decision-making behavior that would often be handled by conditional statements in embedded software.

Adjustable sensitivity

The breadboard implementation included adjustable components that allowed the switching thresholds of the sensing stages to be tuned during testing.

This was useful because environmental conditions and component tolerances could affect the actual sensor voltage. Being able to tune the reference level made it easier to test and adjust the behavior of the prototype.

Why use two sensing methods?

Using both temperature and optical sensing demonstrated how multiple types of sensor information can be incorporated into the same detection system.

A temperature sensor responds to heat, while an optical sensor reacts to changes in the amount of light reaching it. Each measurement captures a different environmental condition.

Although this project was an educational prototype rather than a commercial fire alarm, combining multiple sensor inputs provided useful experience working with different analog sensing methods in one system.

Design Focus

Turning physical conditions into circuit decisions.

The main engineering challenge was translating changes in temperature and light into reliable electrical signals, setting useful switching thresholds, and combining the sensing stages into an analog warning system.

Testing the prototype

The circuit was tested directly on the breadboard by changing the conditions around the sensors and observing the output response.

The thermistor could be exposed to a warmer condition to observe how the temperature-sensing stage responded.

For the optical stage, covering the photoresistor reduced the light reaching the device and provided an easy way to verify whether the circuit responded to the intended change in sensor resistance.

The adjustable thresholds were then tuned until the detection stages responded consistently to the intended test conditions.

Engineering challenges

One challenge was getting the sensor signals and reference voltages into useful ranges. Analog sensors do not provide a simple digital on/off output, so changes in component values and environmental conditions can affect how the system behaves.

Breadboard wiring also required careful troubleshooting. With multiple analog stages connected together, an incorrect connection or unexpected voltage at one stage could affect the operation of the entire circuit.

The project reinforced the importance of measuring voltages at different points in the circuit instead of trying to troubleshoot the whole system at once.

What I learned

This project strengthened my understanding of analog sensor interfaces, voltage dividers, threshold detection, analog signal processing, and breadboard prototyping.

It also helped me better understand the relationship between a physical sensor and the electrical signal ultimately used by a control circuit.

Compared with my microcontroller-based projects, this circuit was a useful reminder that sensing, decision-making, and control can all be implemented directly in hardware without software.

This project was an educational analog prototype. The photoresistor was used to create a controllable optical input during testing and should not be interpreted as a validated commercial smoke-detection method.
