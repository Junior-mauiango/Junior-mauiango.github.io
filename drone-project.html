Project overview

This project is the development of a custom quadcopter flight-control system rather than relying on a prebuilt commercial flight controller. The goal is to build the control stack from the microcontroller level up, including sensor communication, attitude estimation, pilot input, motor output, stabilization, and eventually higher-level safety and autonomous functions.

The project uses an STM32 microcontroller as the primary flight controller. The controller reads inertial measurements from the IMU, determines the aircraft's orientation, processes pilot commands, and continuously adjusts the four motor outputs to oppose unwanted roll, pitch, and yaw motion.

Development has progressed from individual peripheral and motor tests to integrated propeller-on stabilization testing. The current focus is improving stability under real motor vibration and tuning the control response before attempting sustained free flight.

System architecture

The quadcopter is organized around a real-time feedback loop. Pilot commands provide the desired aircraft behavior, while the IMU provides feedback about the aircraft's actual motion. The STM32 compares the desired and measured states, calculates corrections, and modifies the motor speeds through the ESCs.

Sense

Read accelerometer and gyroscope measurements from the IMU and process them into usable flight-state information.

Calculate

Compare the commanded and measured attitude and calculate stabilization corrections using PID control.

Actuate

Mix the control corrections across four motors and update ESC PWM commands to generate the required torque.

Hardware

The airframe uses four brushless motors, each driven independently by an electronic speed controller. The STM32 produces the control signals for the ESCs while the flight battery supplies the propulsion system. An IMU mounted near the center of the aircraft provides acceleration and angular-rate measurements used by the stabilization loop.

A radio receiver provides pilot throttle, roll, pitch, and yaw commands. These inputs are processed by the STM32 and converted into control targets rather than being sent directly to the motors. This allows the flight controller to continuously correct the aircraft even while responding to pilot input.

IMU communication and attitude sensing

One of the first major stages of development was establishing reliable communication between the STM32 and the IMU. Accelerometer and gyroscope data are read continuously and converted from raw sensor values into measurements that can be used by the control system.

Sensor calibration is important because even a stationary IMU can contain offsets that would otherwise appear to the controller as real motion. Calibration and filtering are therefore used before the measurements enter the stabilization system.

The processed accelerometer and gyroscope information is then used for attitude estimation. Gyroscope measurements provide fast rotational response, while accelerometer information provides a longer-term reference for roll and pitch.

Motor and ESC control

Each of the four motors is controlled independently through its ESC. The STM32 generates PWM output signals that command the ESCs, allowing the software to vary each motor's speed independently.

Independent motor control is essential because a quadcopter does not use conventional control surfaces. Roll, pitch, and yaw are produced by deliberately changing the thrust generated by different motors. The flight controller therefore converts stabilization corrections into four individual motor commands through a motor-mixing stage.

PID stabilization

The stabilization controller calculates the difference between the desired state and the measured state. A PID controller then converts that error into a correction that can be applied to the motors.

The proportional term responds directly to the current error. The integral term can compensate for persistent bias, while the derivative term can help oppose rapid changes and improve damping. These terms must be tuned carefully because an aggressive controller can cause oscillation while a controller with insufficient gain may respond too slowly to disturbances.

The resulting roll, pitch, and yaw corrections are combined with the base throttle command through the motor mixer. Depending on the direction of the required correction, some motors are accelerated while others are reduced.

From bench testing to propellers

Development was performed incrementally rather than assembling the entire aircraft and immediately attempting flight. Individual motor control was verified first, followed by sensor readings, receiver input, attitude estimation, and stabilization behavior.

A particularly important test was verifying the direction of the feedback correction. When the aircraft is manually tilted in one direction, the controller must change the appropriate motor outputs to generate torque in the opposite direction. An incorrect sign or motor mapping can make the controller amplify a disturbance instead of correcting it.

Current engineering challenge

The largest difference between the earlier bench tests and the current propeller-on tests is vibration. With the propulsion system running, the IMU is exposed to mechanical vibration from the motors, propellers, frame, and mounting structure. These disturbances can enter the sensor measurements and cause the estimated attitude and PID corrections to fluctuate.

During current testing, motor commands fluctuate more as throttle is increased, and the aircraft has shown a tendency to roll toward the right. The cause is still being investigated rather than treated as a solved problem.

The debugging process involves separating possible software and hardware causes: PID gains, sensor noise, IMU mounting, frame vibration, motor/propeller balance, motor direction, motor mixing, thrust differences, and the aircraft's physical weight distribution. This stage is providing the transition from a controller that behaves correctly in software to one that remains stable on a real airframe.

Safety and arming

Safety is a major part of the flight-controller design because incorrect motor commands can immediately become hazardous once propellers are installed. Motor output is therefore being developed alongside arming and safe-state behavior rather than treating safety as a feature to add only after flight control is complete.

The longer-term design also includes failsafe behavior for conditions such as communication loss and low battery voltage. The intended goal is for these conditions to trigger controlled descent behavior instead of allowing the aircraft to continue operating without a valid control link or adequate battery capacity.

Planned development

The immediate priority is achieving stable manual flight and tuning the attitude controller. Higher-level functionality will be added only after the fundamental stabilization loop is reliable.

  • PID tuning: refine roll, pitch, and yaw response under real flight conditions.
  • Vibration reduction: improve the quality of IMU measurements during higher motor speeds.
  • Stable hover: progress from restrained testing to controlled low-altitude flight.
  • Altitude sensing: integrate pressure-based altitude measurement after attitude stabilization is reliable.
  • Altitude hold: develop a closed-loop vertical control system capable of maintaining a target altitude.
  • Battery monitoring: monitor LiPo voltage and provide the flight controller with battery-state information.
  • Communication-loss failsafe: detect loss of valid pilot commands and transition the aircraft into a safe response.
  • Low-battery landing: develop controlled descent behavior when battery voltage reaches a defined safety threshold.

What I am learning

The project combines several areas of embedded engineering in one system: microcontroller peripherals, real-time sensor acquisition, digital communication, feedback control, power electronics, motor control, signal filtering, debugging, and physical system integration.

One of the most important lessons has been that a control algorithm working correctly on the bench does not automatically mean the aircraft will be stable. Sensor noise, vibration, motor differences, frame dynamics, timing, and controller tuning all interact once the propellers are running.

That makes the current testing stage particularly valuable: the project has moved beyond simply getting individual components to work and into integrating those components into a complete real-time control system.
