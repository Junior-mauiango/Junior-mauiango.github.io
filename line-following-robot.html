Project overview

This project challenged me to build a mobile robot that could respond to its environment without relying on programmable control. Instead of using a microcontroller, the robot's behavior was created through physical electronic circuitry.

The objective was to build a robot capable of detecting and following a black-line course using an analog control approach. The project required sensor integration, motor-control circuitry, breadboard prototyping, repeated testing, and hardware troubleshooting.

The design evolved throughout development as different circuit approaches were tested and modified based on the behavior of the physical robot.

Purely analog control

One of the most important aspects of the project was that the robot did not use software, firmware, or a microcontroller.

The decisions normally handled by embedded code instead had to be implemented directly in hardware. Sensor behavior and motor-control signals were produced through the analog circuitry mounted on the robot.

Working this way required me to think directly about electrical signals, switching behavior, component interactions, physical sensor placement, and the response of the motors to changes in the circuit.

Design development

The circuit changed as the robot was developed and tested. Earlier design approaches were explored before the control circuitry was simplified and adjusted for the final physical robot.

This iterative process became an important part of the project. Instead of treating the first circuit as the final design, each stage was tested on the physical robot and modified when the behavior did not meet the requirements.

The result was a design process driven by experimentation, troubleshooting, and practical hardware performance.

Design Iteration

Testing shaped the final design.

The circuit evolved through multiple versions while the robot was being built and tested. Simplifying parts of the design reduced wiring, made troubleshooting easier, and helped improve the reliability of the physical robot.

Line following

With the sensing and motor-control stages working together, the robot could respond as it moved along the black-line course.

When the robot began moving away from the desired path, changes in the sensor signals altered the motor-control behavior and allowed the robot to steer back toward the line.

Because the behavior was produced without software, reliable operation depended heavily on the physical circuit, component behavior, sensor placement, and repeated calibration.

Sensor calibration

One of the practical challenges was obtaining consistent detection between the black line and the surrounding driving surface.

The sensors had to be positioned so that the control circuitry could reliably distinguish changes in the track. Small physical changes in sensor placement could noticeably affect how the robot responded.

The sensing system was therefore adjusted through repeated physical testing until the robot could follow the course consistently.

Hardware troubleshooting

Breadboard complexity became increasingly important as the project developed. More wiring and components made it harder to isolate problems when the robot did not behave as expected.

Troubleshooting required checking the circuit in stages instead of treating the robot as one complete system. Sensor behavior, motor-control signals, wiring, power connections, and mechanical movement all had to be evaluated separately.

This made the project a useful exercise in systematic hardware debugging rather than simply circuit assembly.

Engineering challenges

The project involved considerably more troubleshooting than simply connecting sensors to motors.

Breadboard complexity became a major consideration as the circuit grew. Increasing amounts of wiring made diagnosing failures more difficult and reinforced the importance of keeping hardware designs as simple as possible.

Sensor reliability was another challenge. Since the robot relied entirely on physical sensing and analog circuitry, poor calibration could cause incorrect steering or prevent the robot from responding correctly to the line.

The interaction between the electronics and mechanical platform was also important. A circuit could behave correctly electrically while the overall robot still needed adjustments to sensor placement, motor response, or physical alignment.

What I learned

This project gave me practical experience creating control behavior without relying on software. Instead of writing decisions in code, the robot's behavior had to emerge directly from its electronic hardware.

It strengthened my understanding of analog sensing, motor-control circuits, breadboard prototyping, circuit testing, calibration, and systematic hardware troubleshooting.

The project also reinforced an important design principle: the most complex circuit is not necessarily the best solution. Simplifying a design can make it easier to understand, debug, and improve.
